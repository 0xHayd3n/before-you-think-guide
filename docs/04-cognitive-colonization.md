# 4. Cognitive colonization

## A proposed diagnostic profile

The paper's central contribution is the idea that a deeply integrated cognitive support can carry goals or optimization criteria set by someone else. If those criteria shape thought without being apparent to the person, and the person would not endorse them after reflection, the authors call the relationship cognitive colonization. They explicitly caution that the term is a theoretical construct, not an already isolated empirical kind. [§5, pp. 17–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13658v1#page=17)

The metaphor points to direction and governance, not merely to influence or assistance. A system can successfully complete a task while also shaping which tasks, options, or preferences feel desirable. The concern is that a person's ordinary process of choosing can come to include objectives they did not author and would not accept.

## Reading the prose and Table 3 together

The prose introduces constitutive integration, external direction, normative misalignment, and opacity. Table 3 makes **downstream incorporation** explicit and groups its rows into descriptive and normative conditions. These should be read as connected parts of a profile, not as separate diagnostic tests. [§5 and Table 3, pp. 18–22](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13658v1#page=18)

| Part of the proposed profile | What would need to be shown | What is insufficient on its own |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Constitutive integration and downstream incorporation | The support is woven into cognitive routines, and its shaping is taken up as the person's own rather than remaining an acknowledged suggestion | Frequent use, high satisfaction, or an occasional accepted answer |
| Exogenous directional governance | Relevant biases or goals originate in external design, training, incentives, or infrastructure | Merely noting that another organization wrote the software |
| Opacity | The directional influence is not available to the person for recognition and evaluation at the point of use | A complex model without evidence about what the person can understand or contest |
| Misalignment with reflective endorsement | The person would reject the relevant shaping under meaningful reflection | An observer's dislike of the person's decision, or a mismatch with a designer's preferred values |

This is a condensed interpretive aid to the diagnostic, not a validated screening scale. It also highlights why the claim is demanding: evidence for one row does not automatically satisfy the others.

## Why persistence matters, and what it does not establish

The paper discusses AI-related bias that persists when assistance is removed and bias amplification larger than a human-adviser comparison. It treats such findings as important evidence of incorporation. [§5, pp. 18–19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13658v1#page=18)

A persistent effect can make an explanation based only on immediate compliance incomplete. It does not establish the complete colonization profile. People routinely learn, form habits, and revise beliefs after outside input. A discriminating account must explain why the influence is constitutive, how externally chosen objectives govern it, and why the person would not endorse it. [Module 7](07-questions-and-research-agenda.md) offers questions for designing such a study.

## The normative hinge: functional success is not endorsement

Section 5.1 argues that the extended mind thesis establishes a kind of functional integration; it does not guarantee that the integrated component serves the person's reflective purposes. A layer can improve task execution and still direct attention, preferences, or habits toward someone else's ends. The authors call this functional alignment combined with normative misalignment. [§5.1, pp. 22–23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13658v1#page=22)

Consider an original illustrative case, not evidence from the paper: an assistant efficiently recommends convenient articles, and the person relies on its selection as the default starting point for thought. The efficiency is a functional success. If selection is optimized to keep the person engaged while quietly narrowing topics they would prefer to explore more broadly, there may be a normative mismatch. Calling this colonization would still require evidence of integration, opaque external direction, and actual reflective non-endorsement.

## The “part of me” objection

The paper raises an objection to its own position: if the system is part of the person's cognition, how can it also threaten the person from within? Its answer draws a distinction between **integration** and **nativeness**. Internal biases can also conflict with reflective aims, but the authors treat System 1 as native to the biological cognitive architecture and System 0 as integrated on a non-biological substrate. [§5.1, pp. 23–24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13658v1#page=23)

This is a philosophical boundary, not a result of testing different substrates. The authors maintain that even very early, deep AI integration does not erase its externally implemented origin. A reader can accept the possibility of harmful integrated supports while questioning whether this native/non-native distinction is necessary or adequately defended. The paper's citizenship analogy is an argumentative illustration, not empirical support.

## What follows for intervention?

The conclusion connects different diagnoses with different remedies. Deference suggests verification and calibrated trust. Ecological convergence suggests plural information environments. The colonization account suggests that both may be insufficient if the problem is built into the person's process of forming judgments. [§6, pp. 25–26](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13658v1#page=25)

The paper does not test those remedies, establish their effect sizes, or provide an operational governance program. A practical reader should preserve the distinction between an argument that additional safeguards are needed and evidence that a particular safeguard works.
